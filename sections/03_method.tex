This section develops the mechanism used in the rest of the paper. We do not
attempt to prove full Neural Collapse convergence for deep networks. Existing
work already shows that optimizer choice and weight-decay coupling can change
Neural Collapse behavior \citep{zhao2026optimizer,loshchilov2019decoupled}. Our
goal is different: we use optimizer update rules to derive testable predictions
about the penultimate feature geometry that OOD detectors read. The resulting
framework connects three objects:
optimization rules, detector-relevant geometry channels, and diagnostic controls.

\subsection{Setup and claim boundary}
\label{subsec:setup-claim-boundary}

We consider a \(K\)-class classifier decomposed into a penultimate feature map
and a linear classifier,
\begin{equation}
    z(x)=Wh(x)+b,
    \label{eq:classifier-decomposition}
\end{equation}
where \(h(x)\in\mathbb{R}^d\), \(W\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times d}\), and
\(z(x)\in\mathbb{R}^K\). Let \(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}\) be the ID training
set and let \(\mathcal{D}_c\subset\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}\) denote the subset
of class \(c\). For a trained model, define the class mean
\begin{equation}
    \mu_c
    =
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_c|}
    \sum_{(x_i,y_i)\in\mathcal{D}_c}
    h(x_i),
    \label{eq:class-mean-section3}
\end{equation}
and decompose each feature into a class mean and a residual,
\begin{equation}
    h_i = \mu_{y_i}+\varepsilon_i.
    \label{eq:feature-residual-decomposition}
\end{equation}
The class-conditional covariance is
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_c
    =
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_c|-1}
    \sum_{(x_i,y_i)\in\mathcal{D}_c}
    \varepsilon_i\varepsilon_i^\top,
    \label{eq:class-covariance-section3}
\end{equation}
and the pooled within-class covariance is
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_W
    =
    \frac{1}{K}
    \sum_{c=1}^K \Sigma_c.
    \label{eq:pooled-covariance-section3}
\end{equation}

The feature residuals \(\varepsilon_i\), their covariance \(\Sigma_c\), the class
means \(\mu_c\), and the classifier weights \(W\) are the quantities through
which feature-based OOD detectors interact with the trained model. We summarize
them by a geometry fingerprint
\begin{equation}
    G(h,W)
    =
    \big[
    G_{\mathrm{NC}},
    G_{\mathrm{norm}},
    G_{\mathrm{cov}},
    G_{\mathrm{local}},
    G_{\mathrm{subspace}}
    \big].
    \label{eq:geometry-fingerprint-section3}
\end{equation}
Here \(G_{\mathrm{NC}}\) contains Neural-Collapse-related quantities such as
NC0--NC3 and classifier--feature alignment; \(G_{\mathrm{norm}}\) contains
feature-norm statistics; \(G_{\mathrm{cov}}\) contains within-class dispersion,
covariance spectra, anisotropy, condition number, and effective rank;
\(G_{\mathrm{local}}\) contains local feature-bank neighborhood statistics; and
\(G_{\mathrm{subspace}}\) contains PCA, residual, and class-mean-subspace
structure.

This section provides a mechanistic framework rather than a complete convergence
theory. The analysis uses local linearization or peeled-feature approximations
to identify which geometry channels should move under different optimizers.
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} then tests these predictions across architectures,
optimizers, detectors, and diagnostic controls.

\subsection{Optimizer update rules as geometry interventions}
\label{subsec:optimizer-update-geometry}

We treat optimizer choice as a training-side intervention on the geometry in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:geometry-fingerprint-section3}. The main optimizer families are
SGD, SGDW, Adam, and AdamW. This set separates two axes that are often
confounded: adaptive preconditioning and weight-decay coupling.

For a fixed total weight decay \(\lambda_{\mathrm{total}}\), define an
interpolation between coupled and decoupled decay:
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_{\mathrm{coupled}}
    =
    \gamma\lambda_{\mathrm{total}},
    \qquad
    \lambda_{\mathrm{decoupled}}
    =
    (1-\gamma)\lambda_{\mathrm{total}},
    \qquad
    \gamma\in[0,1].
    \label{eq:coupled-decoupled-interpolation-section3}
\end{equation}
The coupled term is included in the gradient update, whereas the decoupled term
is applied as a separate parameter shrinkage. For a parameter block \(\theta\),
we write the abstract update as
\begin{equation}
    g_t^{(\gamma)}
    =
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta_t)
    +
    \lambda_{\mathrm{coupled}}\theta_t,
    \label{eq:coupled-gradient-section3}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \theta_{t+1}
    =
    (1-\eta_t\lambda_{\mathrm{decoupled}})\theta_t
    -
    \eta_t\mathcal{P}_t(g_t^{(\gamma)}),
    \label{eq:unified-optimizer-update-section3}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{P}_t\) denotes the optimizer-specific momentum or
preconditioning operator. In the Adam family, \(\gamma=0\) corresponds to AdamW
and \(\gamma=1\) corresponds to Adam with coupled L2 regularization.

\paragraph{Proposition 1: coupled and decoupled decay differ under adaptive preconditioning.}
For a non-adaptive optimizer without momentum, coupled L2 decay and decoupled
weight decay induce the same first-order update:
\begin{equation}
    \theta_{t+1}
    =
    \theta_t-\eta_t\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}(\theta_t)-\eta_t\lambda\theta_t.
    \label{eq:sgd-sgdw-equivalence-section3}
\end{equation}
Under an adaptive diagonal preconditioner \(D_t\), however, Adam and AdamW differ
in whether the regularization term is preconditioned:
\begin{equation}
    \theta_{t+1}^{\mathrm{Adam}}
    \approx
    \theta_t-\eta_tD_t\bigl(\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}(\theta_t)+\lambda\theta_t\bigr),
    \label{eq:adam-coupled-preconditioned-section3}
\end{equation}
whereas
\begin{equation}
    \theta_{t+1}^{\mathrm{AdamW}}
    \approx
    (1-\eta_t\lambda)\theta_t-\eta_tD_t\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}(\theta_t).
    \label{eq:adamw-decoupled-preconditioned-section3}
\end{equation}
Thus Adam applies weight decay in the preconditioned geometry, while AdamW
applies weight decay in raw parameter space.

This proposition is not intended as a new Neural Collapse theorem. Rather, it
identifies the update-level mechanism that can create different feature
geometries even when the scalar weight decay and ID accuracy are comparable. In
particular, the AdamW-to-Adam interpolation in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:coupled-decoupled-interpolation-section3} provides a controlled
path for testing whether geometry and detector behavior move along the
weight-decay-coupling axis.

\subsection{Penultimate residual and covariance dynamics}
\label{subsec:residual-covariance-dynamics}

We now connect the optimizer update to penultimate feature geometry. Consider a
terminal-phase or locally linearized regime in which the feature residual
\(\varepsilon_i\) of a class-\(c\) sample evolves approximately under a local
curvature operator \(B_{c,t}\):
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_{h_i}\mathcal{L}
    \approx
    B_{c,t}\varepsilon_{i,t}.
    \label{eq:feature-gradient-local-linearization}
\end{equation}
This approximation is exact in a simple peeled-feature quadratic model and is
used here as a first-order description of the detector-relevant residual
geometry in deep networks.

Under this approximation, the residual update takes different forms under
different optimizers. For SGD or SGDW,
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon_{i,t+1}^{\mathrm{SGD/SGDW}}
    \approx
    \bigl(I-\eta_t(B_{c,t}+\lambda I)\bigr)\varepsilon_{i,t}.
    \label{eq:residual-sgd-section3}
\end{equation}
For coupled Adam,
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon_{i,t+1}^{\mathrm{Adam}}
    \approx
    \bigl(I-\eta_tD_t^h(B_{c,t}+\lambda I)\bigr)\varepsilon_{i,t},
    \label{eq:residual-adam-section3}
\end{equation}
where \(D_t^h\) is the effective feature-side preconditioner induced by the
optimizer and the local Jacobian. For AdamW,
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon_{i,t+1}^{\mathrm{AdamW}}
    \approx
    \bigl(I-\eta_t(\lambda I+D_t^hB_{c,t})\bigr)\varepsilon_{i,t}.
    \label{eq:residual-adamw-section3}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Proposition 2: optimizer-specific covariance recurrence.}
Under the local residual approximation above, the within-class covariance evolves
as
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_{c,t+1}
    \approx
    M_{c,t}\Sigma_{c,t}M_{c,t}^{\top},
    \label{eq:covariance-recurrence-section3}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    M_{c,t}^{\mathrm{SGD/SGDW}}
    =
    I-\eta_t(B_{c,t}+\lambda I),
    \label{eq:m-sgd-section3}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    M_{c,t}^{\mathrm{Adam}}
    =
    I-\eta_tD_t^h(B_{c,t}+\lambda I),
    \label{eq:m-adam-section3}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    M_{c,t}^{\mathrm{AdamW}}
    =
    I-\eta_t(\lambda I+D_t^hB_{c,t}).
    \label{eq:m-adamw-section3}
\end{equation}
Consequently, Adam and AdamW can contract the same residual directions at
different rates. These direction-dependent contraction rates can change
within-class dispersion, covariance eigenspectra, anisotropy, effective rank,
and feature-norm dispersion, even when the final ID accuracy is similar.

This proposition gives the main optimizer-to-geometry bridge in the paper. It
predicts that optimizer differences should not be visible only in scalar NC
metrics. They should also appear in the covariance spectrum, feature norm
statistics, local feature-bank spacing, and subspace structure. These are exactly
the quantities that feature-based OOD detectors read.

\subsection{Geometry channels read by OOD detectors}
\label{subsec:geometry-channels-detectors}

The preceding subsection explains why optimizer choice can move feature
geometry. We now identify how those geometry changes enter common detector
families. Table~\ref{tab:readout-map} summarizes the detector families and the
channels they read.

\paragraph{Distance and neighborhood readouts.}
Raw Euclidean distances mix radial feature norm and angular structure. Let
\(h=ru\) and \(h'=r'u'\), where \(\|u\|_2=\|u'\|_2=1\). Then
\begin{equation}
    \|h-h'\|_2^2
    =
    r^2+(r')^2-2rr'\langle u,u'\rangle.
    \label{eq:raw-distance-radial-angular-section3}
\end{equation}
After L2 normalization,
\begin{equation}
    \|\widehat h-\widehat h'\|_2^2
    =
    2-2\langle u,u'\rangle,
    \qquad
    \widehat h=\frac{h}{\|h\|_2+\epsilon}.
    \label{eq:l2-distance-angular-section3}
\end{equation}
Thus raw kNN and raw distance-based detectors read both feature norm and angular
neighborhood structure, whereas L2-normalized variants remove the radial channel.
This makes raw and L2-normalized versions useful as a diagnostic pair: a large
raw gap that disappears after L2 normalization indicates a radial or
norm-coupled failure channel.

\paragraph{Precision and density readouts.}
Mahalanobis scoring uses a precision-weighted distance:
\begin{equation}
    S_{\mathrm{Maha}}(x)
    =
    -
    \min_c
    (h(x)-\mu_c)^\top
    \Sigma_W^{-1}
    (h(x)-\mu_c).
    \label{eq:maha-score-section3}
\end{equation}
DDU-style Gaussian feature-density scoring uses class-conditional densities:
\begin{equation}
    S_{\mathrm{GMM}}(x)
    =
    \log
    \sum_{c=1}^{K}
    \pi_c
    \mathcal{N}\bigl(h(x)\mid\mu_c,\widehat{\Sigma}_c\bigr).
    \label{eq:gmm-score-section3}
\end{equation}
For a fixed class component, the covariance-sensitive part is
\begin{equation}
    -\frac{1}{2}
    (h-\mu_c)^\top
    \Sigma_c^{-1}
    (h-\mu_c)
    -
    \frac{1}{2}\log|\Sigma_c|.
    \label{eq:gmm-quadratic-logdet-section3}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Proposition 3: covariance expansion reduces precision-weighted penalties.}
If \(A^{(1)}\succeq A^{(0)}\succ 0\), then
\begin{equation}
    (A^{(1)})^{-1}\preceq (A^{(0)})^{-1}.
    \label{eq:inverse-order-section3}
\end{equation}
Therefore, for any residual vector \(\delta\),
\begin{equation}
    \delta^\top(A^{(1)})^{-1}\delta
    \le
    \delta^\top(A^{(0)})^{-1}\delta.
    \label{eq:precision-penalty-section3}
\end{equation}
Thus, if an optimizer expands detector-relevant covariance directions, samples
aligned with those directions receive smaller Mahalanobis or Gaussian quadratic
penalties and can become more ID-like under feature-density scores.

The effect is direction dependent. In whitened coordinates, write
\begin{equation}
    A^{(1)}
    =
    (A^{(0)})^{1/2}
    U\operatorname{diag}(\beta_i)U^\top
    (A^{(0)})^{1/2},
    \qquad
    \beta_i\ge 1.
    \label{eq:whitened-cov-expansion-section3}
\end{equation}
With \(\widetilde{\delta}=U^\top(A^{(0)})^{-1/2}\delta\),
\begin{equation}
    \delta^\top(A^{(1)})^{-1}\delta
    =
    \sum_i
    \frac{\widetilde{\delta}_i^2}{\beta_i}.
    \label{eq:directional-penalty-section3}
\end{equation}
The reduction from the original penalty is
\begin{equation}
    \sum_i
    \left(1-\frac{1}{\beta_i}\right)
    \widetilde{\delta}_i^2.
    \label{eq:directional-penalty-reduction-section3}
\end{equation}
Thus two samples can shift by different amounts depending on their alignment
with expanded covariance directions. This is why covariance changes can alter
OOD ranking rather than merely rescale all scores uniformly.

\paragraph{Angular and subspace readouts.}
Angular and collapse-based detectors read different geometry channels. A generic
prototype or CTM-style angular readout has the form
\begin{equation}
    S_{\mathrm{ang}}(x)
    =
    \max_c
    \left\langle
    \frac{h(x)}{\|h(x)\|_2+\epsilon},
    a_c
    \right\rangle,
    \qquad
    \|a_c\|_2=1,
    \label{eq:angular-readout-section3}
\end{equation}
where \(a_c\) may be a normalized class prototype, a class-typical direction, or
a normalized classifier direction. Such scores are less sensitive to global
feature norm but sensitive to angular class separation and classifier--prototype
alignment.

A residual-subspace readout has the form
\begin{equation}
    S_{\mathrm{res}}(x)
    =
    -
    \|(I-P)h(x)\|_2^2,
    \label{eq:residual-subspace-score-section3}
\end{equation}
where \(P\) projects onto an ID principal subspace, a class-mean subspace, or a
detector-specific subspace. Optimizer-induced changes in effective rank,
principal directions, or residual energy can therefore change subspace-based OOD
scores even when raw distances or class means appear stable.

\subsection{Diagnostic controls and geometry-aware recipe}
\label{subsec:diagnostic-recipe}

The preceding analysis suggests that detector failures should be diagnosed by
the geometry channel that carries them, not by the detector name alone. We
therefore use three families of diagnostic controls.

First, L2 normalization removes the radial channel:
\begin{equation}
    \widehat h(x)
    =
    \frac{h(x)}{\|h(x)\|_2+\epsilon}.
    \label{eq:l2-normalization-section3}
\end{equation}
Recovery after L2 normalization indicates that feature norm or radial
heterogeneity is a major contributor to the raw detector gap.

Second, covariance controls probe whether failure comes from covariance
estimation or conditioning. For shrinkage,
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\Sigma}_{c,\alpha}
    =
    (1-\alpha)\widehat{\Sigma}_c
    +
    \alpha
    \frac{\operatorname{tr}(\widehat{\Sigma}_c)}{d}I.
    \label{eq:shrinkage-section3}
\end{equation}
If shrinkage or diagonalization recovers a detector gap, covariance estimation
or conditioning is likely involved. If it does not, the mismatch is more likely
to involve broader geometry such as norm dispersion, class overlap, or subspace
structure.

Third, PCA and residual-subspace controls test whether nuisance or residual
directions carry the detector gap. Let \(P_k\) be a projector onto the top
\(k\) ID principal components. We compare scores computed on \(P_k h(x)\),
class-conditional projections, and residuals \((I-P_k)h(x)\). These controls
separate principal ID structure from residual directions.

These controls define a geometry-aware diagnostic recipe:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Compute the geometry fingerprint \(G(h,W)\) from ID training features.
    \item Compare raw and L2-normalized distance or neighborhood scores.
    \item Compare full, tied, diagonal, and shrinkage covariance density scores.
    \item Compare raw, PCA-projected, and residual-subspace scores.
    \item Select or stress-test detector families according to the active
    geometry channel.
\end{enumerate}
For example, large feature-norm dispersion together with strong L2 recovery
suggests using L2-Mahalanobis or L2-kNN. Large covariance condition number with
shrinkage recovery suggests a covariance-estimation failure channel. Strong
class-mean alignment and stable prototype geometry suggest that angular or
collapse-based detectors may be more compatible. High effective rank or large
residual energy suggests checking PCA or residual-subspace readouts.

\subsection{Testable predictions}
\label{subsec:testable-predictions}

The framework yields concrete predictions for the experiments.

\paragraph{Prediction 1: weight-decay coupling should move geometry.}
In an AdamW-to-Adam interpolation, ID accuracy may remain comparable while
NC-related metrics, feature-norm statistics, covariance spectra, and detector
behavior move along the coupling axis.

\paragraph{Prediction 2: adaptive coupling gaps should be larger than non-adaptive gaps.}
Because coupled and decoupled decay are much closer under non-adaptive SGD than
under adaptive preconditioning, the SGD--SGDW geometry gap should generally be
smaller than the Adam--AdamW gap under comparable training conditions.

\paragraph{Prediction 3: radial geometry should explain raw distance failures.}
When optimizer choice increases feature-norm dispersion or class-dependent norm
variation, raw Mahalanobis and raw kNN should degrade more than their
L2-normalized counterparts. L2 recovery should be strongest in regimes where
the raw gap is radial or norm-coupled.

\paragraph{Prediction 4: covariance geometry should explain density failures.}
When optimizer choice changes covariance spectra, anisotropy, condition number,
or effective rank, Mahalanobis and DDU-style Gaussian density scores should
change accordingly. Shrinkage, diagonalization, and PCA controls should reveal
whether the failure is due to covariance estimation or a broader geometry
mismatch.

\paragraph{Prediction 5: subspace and prototype geometry should explain angular and NC-based detectors.}
When class-mean geometry and classifier alignment are stable, angular and
collapse-based readouts should remain compatible. When effective rank or
residual energy changes, PCA-based and NECO-style residual diagnostics should be
more sensitive.

Together, these predictions make the theory falsifiable. If optimizer choice
changes accuracy but not the predicted geometry channels, the mechanism is not
supported. If geometry changes but detector families do not respond according to
their readout channel, the compatibility view is incomplete. The experiments in
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} are designed to test both links.
