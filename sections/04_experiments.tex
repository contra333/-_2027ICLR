\subsection{Experimental Protocol and Scope}
\label{subsec:experimental-protocol-scope}

The experiments are designed as a prediction-driven diagnostic study rather than
an exhaustive OOD benchmark. Section~\ref{sec:method} predicts that optimizer
choice changes detector-relevant penultimate geometry, and that detector
families respond according to the geometry channel they read. We therefore
organize the experiments around six questions: whether weight-decay coupling
moves geometry, whether adaptive optimizers amplify the coupling gap, whether
radial geometry explains raw distance failures, whether covariance controls
localize density failures, whether prototype and subspace detectors are stable
in aligned regimes, and whether ID geometry can recommend detector diagnostics.

\textbf{Placeholder status.}
The numeric values in this section are synthetic placeholders used only to
stabilize the paper layout and figure/table pipeline. They are not experimental
evidence and must be replaced with measured values before any submission.

We use CIFAR-10 as the primary ID dataset and evaluate near-OOD and far-OOD
settings separately. The main architecture families are non-residual VGG-style
CNNs, residual ResNet/WideResNet models, and modern ConvNeXt backbones. ViT and
Swin are reserved for appendix robustness experiments because pretraining,
LayerNorm, and token representations may change the optimizer--geometry
interaction. The optimizer set is SGD, SGDW, Adam, and AdamW, with an optional
AdamW-to-Adam interpolation at fixed total weight decay. Detector families
include logit-based scores, distance-based feature scores, local-neighborhood
scores, DDU-style Gaussian feature-density / GMM scores, and extended angular or
subspace diagnostics when their score conventions are fixed.

All detector scores are oriented so that larger values are more ID-like before
AUROC, AUPR-IN, and FPR95 are computed. Geometry is computed only from ID
training features unless otherwise stated. This ensures that geometry-based
diagnostics do not require OOD validation data.

\subsection{Does Weight-Decay Coupling Move Geometry Without Changing Accuracy?}
\label{subsec:wd-coupling-interpolation-experiment}

We first test Prediction~1 by interpolating between AdamW and Adam at fixed
total weight decay. The interpolation parameter \(\gamma\) controls the fraction
of weight decay applied in the coupled gradient path: \(\gamma=0\) corresponds
to AdamW and \(\gamma=1\) corresponds to Adam. If the mechanism in
Section~\ref{subsec:optimizer-update-geometry} is correct, ID accuracy can
remain comparably high while NC metrics, feature-norm statistics, covariance
spectra, and feature-detector behavior move along the coupling axis.

Figure~\ref{fig:wd-coupling-interpolation} shows the planned visualization. The
left panel tracks ID accuracy, the middle panel tracks geometry summaries, and
the right panel tracks detector-family AUROC. The expected pattern is not that
one endpoint is universally better. Instead, the key test is whether geometry
and detector behavior vary systematically with \(\gamma\) while ID accuracy
changes much less.

If the mechanism holds, increasing \(\gamma\) should produce a smoother geometry
transition than a direct AdamW-versus-Adam comparison. If ID accuracy changes
strongly with \(\gamma\), the interpolation no longer isolates geometry from
basic model quality and should be treated as a hyperparameter sensitivity result
rather than a clean mechanism test. If accuracy is stable but geometry does not
move, then weight-decay coupling is not the dominant geometry intervention in
that setting.

\subsection{Is the Adam--AdamW Gap Larger Than the SGD--SGDW Gap?}
\label{subsec:adaptive-coupling-gap-experiment}

Prediction~2 states that coupled and decoupled decay should be more different
under adaptive preconditioning than under non-adaptive SGD. We test this by
comparing two paired gaps under matched training recipes:
\[
    \Delta_{\mathrm{nonadapt}}
    =
    d\bigl(G_{\mathrm{SGD}},G_{\mathrm{SGDW}}\bigr),
    \qquad
    \Delta_{\mathrm{adapt}}
    =
    d\bigl(G_{\mathrm{Adam}},G_{\mathrm{AdamW}}\bigr),
\]
where \(d(\cdot,\cdot)\) is a standardized distance over the ID geometry
fingerprint. We compute the same paired gap for detector-family outputs.

Figure~\ref{fig:adaptive-coupling-gap} summarizes the planned comparison across
architectures. The expected qualitative outcome is
\(\Delta_{\mathrm{adapt}}>\Delta_{\mathrm{nonadapt}}\), especially for geometry
channels involving covariance spectra, feature norms, NC alignment, and local
feature-bank structure. Failure of this pattern would suggest that decoupling
alone, rather than adaptive preconditioning, is sufficient to alter the
geometry, or that the chosen architecture/training recipe masks the adaptive
coupling gap.

\subsection{Do Detector Families Inherit the Geometry Shift?}
\label{subsec:detector-family-inheritance-experiment}

We next test whether detector families respond according to their readout
channels. Logit-based detectors read output confidence and margins; raw
Mahalanobis and raw kNN read radial distance, class separation, and local
feature-bank structure; DDU-style Gaussian feature-density / GMM scores read
covariance and density; angular and subspace diagnostics read prototype or
residual structure.

Figure~\ref{fig:detector-family-delta-heatmap} reports the planned
optimizer-relative AUROC gap heatmap. The intended reference is the matched SGD
anchor within each architecture and dataset. Positive values indicate that the
detector family improves relative to the anchor, and negative values indicate
degradation. This figure should not be interpreted as an optimizer leaderboard.
The relevant question is whether each detector family changes in the direction
predicted by its geometry readout.

If logit scores remain stable while raw feature-distance scores degrade, the
optimizer has not produced a uniformly worse model. Instead, it has produced a
representation that is less compatible with the raw distance channel. If all
detectors degrade together, the result is more likely to reflect a broad model
quality issue rather than a geometry--detector compatibility effect.

\subsection{Does Radial Geometry Explain Raw Distance Failures?}
\label{subsec:radial-channel-experiment}

Prediction~3 isolates the radial channel. We compare raw Mahalanobis and raw kNN
against their L2-normalized counterparts using the same checkpoints, ID feature
bank, OOD datasets, and score direction convention. L2 normalization removes
feature-norm variation while preserving angular information. Therefore,
recovery after L2 normalization indicates that the raw detector gap is carried
by radial or norm-coupled geometry.

Figure~\ref{fig:l2-recovery-paths} shows the planned paired recovery plot. Each
line connects the raw detector gap to the corresponding L2-normalized gap for
the same optimizer, architecture, and OOD regime. The expected qualitative
outcome is that optimizers with larger feature-norm dispersion show larger raw
distance degradation and stronger L2 recovery.

explain the detector behavior. The remaining gap should then be analyzed through
covariance, local density, class overlap, or subspace structure.

\subsection{Are Covariance Failures Localized by Covariance Controls?}
\label{subsec:covariance-channel-experiment}

Prediction~4 tests whether covariance geometry explains failures of
Mahalanobis-style and DDU-style Gaussian feature-density / GMM readouts. We
compare full, tied, diagonal, shrinkage, and PCA-projected covariance variants.
These interventions are not new detector proposals; they are controls for
identifying whether the failure arises from covariance estimation,
ill-conditioning, anisotropy, or broader feature geometry.

\input{tables/tab_dummy_covariance_controls}

If shrinkage or diagonal covariance reduces the detector gap, the failure likely
involves unstable covariance estimation or ill-conditioning. If PCA projection
helps while shrinkage does not, the failure may be concentrated in nuisance or
residual directions. If none of the covariance controls help, then the mismatch
is likely not a covariance-estimation problem alone; class overlap, radial
geometry, or subspace structure may dominate.

\subsection{Do Prototype and Subspace Diagnostics Track NC-Aligned Regimes?}
\label{subsec:prototype-subspace-experiment}

Prediction~5 concerns angular, prototype, and subspace readouts. These
diagnostics are reported in the main text only when their score conventions are
fixed. Otherwise, they are treated as appendix probes. The planned analysis
compares NC alignment, class-mean angular structure, classifier--feature
alignment, effective rank, PCA explained variance, and residual energy against
CTM-style, NECO-style, prototype-cosine, and residual-subspace scores.

Figure~\ref{fig:prototype-subspace-alignment} shows the planned visualization.
The expected qualitative outcome is that angular or prototype detectors become
more stable when class-mean geometry and classifier alignment are strong, while
residual or PCA-based detectors become more informative when effective rank or
residual energy changes substantially.

\subsection{Can ID Geometry Recommend Detector Diagnostics?}
\label{subsec:geometry-aware-diagnostic-selector}

Prediction~6 asks whether ID-only geometry can recommend a detector family or
diagnostic control before using OOD validation data. We first evaluate a simple
rule-based selector because it is easier to interpret than a learned model. The
rules are intentionally diagnostic: high norm dispersion recommends L2-based
controls, poor covariance conditioning recommends shrinkage or diagonal
covariance, strong NC alignment recommends angular or prototype diagnostics, and
large residual energy recommends PCA or residual-subspace probes.

\input{tables/tab_dummy_geometry_selector}

A learned selector can be evaluated after enough architecture--optimizer--seed
cells are available. The target should not be the single best detector on one
OOD dataset. A more stable target is a detector family or control type that is
statistically competitive across OOD regimes. If the selector fails, then either
the fingerprint \(G(h,W)\) is missing important geometry channels, or OOD
severity information is necessary to choose a detector reliably.

\subsection{Summary of the Experimental Logic}
\label{subsec:experimental-logic-summary}

The experiments are designed to test both links in the
optimizer--geometry--detector pathway. The interpolation and paired optimizer
comparisons test whether optimizer choice moves the predicted ID geometry
channels. The detector-family and diagnostic-control experiments test whether
downstream scores respond according to their readout channel. The
geometry-aware selector then tests whether the same ID geometry fingerprint is
informative before OOD validation. Together, these experiments support or
falsify the compatibility view without requiring the paper to claim that one
optimizer or one detector is universally best.
