Deep neural networks are commonly selected by in-distribution (ID) accuracy.
This criterion is natural for closed-set recognition, but it is not sufficient
for reliable deployment. In open environments, a model must also recognize
ambiguous inputs, low-confidence cases, and samples that do not come from the
training distribution. Out-of-distribution (OOD) detection addresses this problem
by assigning a score that separates ID-like inputs from unreliable or shifted
inputs. However, different OOD scores do not read the same part of a trained
model. Logit-based scores such as maximum softmax probability (MSP), MaxLogit,
and Energy read output confidence, logit scale, and margin structure
\citep{hendrycks2017baseline,liu2020energy}. Feature-based detectors instead
operate on the penultimate representation: Mahalanobis distance reads class
means and covariance, kNN reads local feature-bank neighborhoods, DDU-style
Gaussian density reads class-conditional density, and angular or
collapse-based methods such as CTM and NECO read prototype, subspace, or
neural-collapse-related structure
\citep{lee2018simple,sun2022knn,mukhoti2021ddu,nguyen2023ctm,benammar2023neco}.
Thus, OOD reliability is not determined only by the score formula; it also
depends on the geometry of the representation on which the score is applied.

This observation raises a question that is largely hidden when optimizers are
treated only as tools for improving accuracy. Optimizer choice can change the
implicit bias of training and therefore the geometry of the learned
representation. Recent work on Neural Collapse shows that optimizers and
weight-decay coupling can substantially affect last-layer and penultimate
geometry, with SGD, Adam, and AdamW exhibiting different collapse behavior
\citep{papyan2020prevalence,zhao2026optimizer}. In particular, the distinction
between coupled and decoupled weight decay is not merely an implementation
detail: it can change the dynamics by which class means, classifier weights, and
feature clouds organize during training \citep{loshchilov2019decoupled}. If
feature-based OOD detectors depend on this geometry, then optimizer choice may
affect OOD detectability even when ID accuracy is comparable.

We study this optimizer--geometry--detector interaction. Our central hypothesis
is that optimizer choice induces a penultimate geometry fingerprint, and that
OOD detector performance depends on whether this fingerprint is compatible with
the geometry channel read by the detector. A raw distance-based detector may be
sensitive to radial feature-norm variation; a covariance-based detector may be
sensitive to anisotropy, conditioning, or finite-sample covariance estimation; a
kNN detector may be sensitive to local feature-bank spacing; and an angular or
neural-collapse-based detector may be sensitive to prototype directions,
classifier alignment, or residual subspace energy. Under this view, there need
not be a universally best optimizer or a universally best detector. The relevant
object is the compatibility between the optimizer-induced representation and the
detector-family readout. Figure~\ref{fig:optimizer-geometry-pipeline}
summarizes this optimizer-conditioned geometry compatibility framing.

We evaluate this view using SGD, SGDW, Adam, and AdamW as controlled optimizer
families. This choice separates two axes that are often conflated: adaptivity
and weight-decay coupling. SGD and SGDW compare coupled and decoupled decay in a
non-adaptive optimizer, while Adam and AdamW compare the same distinction under
coordinate-wise adaptive updates. To test whether the resulting geometry effects
are architecture-specific or more general, our main study focuses on
convolutional backbones with different inductive biases: VGG-style networks
without residual connections, ResNet/WideResNet models with residual
connections, and modern ConvNeXt backbones. Transformer-based architectures such
as ViT and Swin are treated as additional robustness settings, where pretraining,
normalization layers, and token-based representations may change how optimizer
effects appear.

A key part of our study is to separate performance baselines from diagnostic
controls. For kNN, L2-normalized features are often the natural deployment
choice; for Mahalanobis-style scoring, recent work also shows that feature
normalization can substantially improve performance. We therefore do not treat
raw kNN or raw Mahalanobis as the preferred final detectors. Instead, we use
them as diagnostic probes. Comparing raw and L2-normalized versions tells us
whether an optimizer-induced detector gap is carried by radial or norm-coupled
geometry. Similarly, covariance shrinkage, diagonal or tied covariance variants,
and PCA-based projection controls are not introduced as new OOD detectors, but
as interventions that localize whether failures arise from covariance
conditioning, anisotropy, finite-sample estimation, nuisance dimensions, or
subspace structure.

This framing differs from a standard OOD benchmark. Rather than asking which
detector wins on average, we ask why detector rankings change when the
representation changes. Representation-centric analyses have shown that OOD
detector competitiveness depends on architecture, training paradigm, source
dataset, and shift severity \citep{olivares2025systematic}. We focus on one
specific and actionable source of such variation: optimizer choice. By measuring
Neural Collapse metrics, feature-norm statistics, within-class dispersion, class
separation, covariance spectra, effective rank, and local neighborhood
structure, we connect optimizer-induced geometry changes to detector-family
behavior.

Our contributions are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate optimizer-conditioned geometry compatibility as a lens for
    feature-based OOD detection: optimizer choice shapes the penultimate geometry
    that downstream detector families read.
    \item We systematically study SGD, SGDW, Adam, and AdamW across convolutional
    architectures with different inductive biases, including non-residual,
    residual, and modern CNN backbones, with transformer-based models used as
    additional robustness settings.
    \item We identify penultimate geometry fingerprints, including
    neural-collapse metrics, feature norms, within-class dispersion, class
    separation, covariance spectra, and local feature-bank structure, as
    mediators between optimizer choice and detector behavior.
    \item We use L2 normalization, covariance shrinkage, and PCA-based controls
    to distinguish radial, covariance, and subspace failure channels, clarifying
    when raw feature-detector failures reflect representation geometry rather
    than detector formula alone.
    \item We propose a geometry-aware diagnostic perspective for selecting or
    stress-testing feature-based OOD detectors: evaluate an optimizer jointly
    with the representation geometry it induces and the detector family that will
    read that geometry.
\end{itemize}
