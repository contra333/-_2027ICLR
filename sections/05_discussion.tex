\subsection{Compatibility, Not Optimizer Ranking}
\label{subsec:compatibility-not-ranking}

The experimental logic should not be read as a universal ranking of optimizers
for feature-based uncertainty. The same optimizer-induced representation can be
favorable for one detector family and unfavorable for another, because each
family reads a different channel of the trained model. Logit-based scores read
output confidence, logit scale, and margin structure. Raw Mahalanobis and kNN
read distances, feature norms, covariance structure, and local neighborhoods in
the penultimate feature space. Thus, an optimizer may produce a representation
that remains compatible with logit readouts while becoming incompatible with raw
feature-distance readouts.

In the current draft, Figures~\ref{fig:detector-family-delta-heatmap} and
\ref{fig:l2-recovery-paths} are synthetic placeholders that specify the intended
diagnostic comparisons. They should be interpreted as layout and analysis
targets, not as evidence. Once measured values replace the placeholders, these
figures should test whether optimization or ID-performance improvements transfer
uniformly to downstream uncertainty readouts. The relevant object is the
compatibility between the geometry produced by the optimizer and the geometry
read by the detector.

This interpretation also clarifies the limitation of accuracy-only model
selection. Models with comparable ID performance can differ in
neural-collapse-related class structure, feature norms, within-class dispersion,
and class separation, as targeted by
Figure~\ref{fig:wd-coupling-interpolation}. These geometry fingerprints may then
be inherited differently by detector families. When feature-based uncertainty is
used, optimizer choice should therefore be treated as a reliability-relevant
design decision rather than a purely training-side detail.

\subsection{What the Diagnostics Identify}
\label{subsec:what-diagnostics-identify}

The diagnostic interventions in this work are not proposed as new detectors or
universal fixes. Their role is to identify which geometry channels are
implicated in detector gaps once real measurements are available. L2
normalization removes the radial feature-norm channel before Mahalanobis or kNN
scoring. Covariance shrinkage changes the conditioning of the covariance
estimate and the precision weights used by inverse-covariance detectors.

The planned L2 recovery analysis in Figure~\ref{fig:l2-recovery-paths} is meant
to test whether raw Mahalanobis or raw kNN gaps shrink after the radial channel
is removed. Such a pattern would suggest that a feature-distance failure is not
merely a generic loss of representation quality, but is tied to a radial or
norm-coupled geometry channel. Since uniform global scaling alone would preserve
the Mahalanobis quadratic form when covariance is fitted consistently, a measured
raw-to-L2 recovery pattern would point to non-uniform geometry changes such as
sample-dependent or class-dependent feature-norm shifts, direction-dependent
spread, or local neighborhood distortion.

Covariance controls give a different diagnostic signal. They can reduce
inverse-covariance sensitivity and stabilize covariance-side estimation, but a
remaining gap after shrinkage or diagonalization would indicate that covariance
conditioning is not the only failure channel. Feature-based detector behavior is
better understood as a multi-channel compatibility problem involving radial
geometry, covariance spectra, class separation, and local density.

The main lesson is therefore not that L2 normalization is the best detector. The
lesson is that L2 recovery narrows the set of plausible failure channels. When a
raw detector gap largely disappears after L2 normalization, radial or
norm-coupled geometry is implicated. When a gap remains after covariance
shrinkage, covariance-side correction alone is insufficient to explain the
mismatch.

\subsection{Limitations and Scope}
\label{subsec:limitations-scope}

At the current draft stage, the Section~\ref{sec:experiments} numbers are
synthetic placeholders. They are useful only for stabilizing the paper layout,
figure pipeline, and prediction-driven organization. The final conclusions must
be based on measured values from the stated architecture--optimizer--seed cells,
and the placeholder numbers must be replaced before submission.

The intended study is controlled but not universal. The conclusions should not
be interpreted as claims over all datasets, architectures, optimizers, or OOD
regimes. The comparison should be accuracy-controlled rather than a claim of
exact ID-accuracy equivalence. Residual differences in ID accuracy or
calibration may contribute to detector behavior and should be reported alongside
geometry and OOD metrics.

The OOD coverage is also limited. CIFAR-100 and TinyImageNet provide useful
near-OOD evaluations, while SVHN and MNIST serve more naturally as far or
easy-far controls. Broader natural-image OOD datasets, CIFAR-100 as an ID
setting, and denser local learning-rate/weight-decay grids would help separate
optimizer-induced geometry effects from representation-quality effects more
sharply.

The detector coverage is intentionally focused. Our goal is not to introduce a
new OOD score or to benchmark every detector family. The main evidence concerns
logit-based scores, raw and L2-normalized feature-distance scores, and
Gaussian feature-density controls. Angular and subspace-oriented readouts should
be treated as extensions unless their implementation and score conventions are
fixed and included in the same evaluation protocol.

Finally, the geometry--detector link targeted here is diagnostic rather than a
complete causal proof. The main figures are designed to test an aligned pattern:
optimizer-selected configurations induce distinct geometry fingerprints,
detector families respond differently to those fingerprints, and diagnostic
controls reveal which geometry channel is implicated. We do not claim that any
single geometry metric alone causes detector degradation. Stronger causal claims
would require tighter ID-accuracy bands, additional architectures and datasets,
and direct interventions that control specific geometry channels.
